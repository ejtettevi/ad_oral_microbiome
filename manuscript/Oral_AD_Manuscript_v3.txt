ABSTRACT
Introduction: While oral microbiome dysbiosis has been implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis, the mechanistic links between oral microbial perturbations and AD pathology remain incompletely understood. 
Methods: Here, we performed high-resolution 16S rRNA sequencing on oral microbiomes from AD patients and age-matched controls, employing a rigorous workflow encompassing amplicon sequence variant inference, taxonomic and functional profiling via PICRUSt2, and machine learning classification. We analyzed diversity metrics, conducted differential abundance testing using ANCOM-BC2 with CLR-transformed data, and evaluated diagnostic potential through Random Forest modeling with cross-validation.
Results: AD cases exhibited significant oral microbiome dysbiosis characterized by a 20% expansion of Proteobacteria and 61% depletion of Actinobacteriota, with enrichment of potentially pathogenic taxa (Bacilli, Gammaproteobacteria) and depletion of beneficial groups (Lactobacillales, Streptococcaceae). Alpha diversity was significantly increased in AD (Chao1 p=0.003; Fisher p=0.009), indicating community destabilization. Functional profiling revealed coordinated upregulation of biosynthetic pathways including peptidoglycan maturation (58% increase), thiamine formation, and phospholipid biosynthesis. Notably, a Random Forest model achieved strong case-control discrimination (AUC=0.85) using microbiome-derived metabolic signatures.
Conclusion: Our integrated analysis demonstrates that AD-associated oral dysbiosis involves multi-layered alterations spanning taxonomic restructuring, increased diversity, and metabolic reprogramming. These findings establish oral microbiome signatures as promising non-invasive biomarkers for AD detection, with potential implications for understanding microbiome-gut-brain axis contributions to neurodegeneration.

Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease; oral microbiome; dysbiosis; metabolic biomarkers

INTRODUCTION
The human oral microbiome serves as a critical interface between microbial ecology and systemic health, with mounting evidence implicating its dysregulation in neurodegenerative pathogenesis, particularly Alzheimer's disease (AD) (Dominy et al., 2019; Olsen & Singhrao, 2019). This complex microbial community maintains dynamic equilibrium through sophisticated ecological networks that profoundly influence host immune surveillance, metabolic homeostasis, and neuroinflammatory cascades (Zhu et al., 2025; Kilian et al., 2016; Long et al., 2022b). Disruption of these microbiome-host interactions—collectively termed dysbiosis—can trigger pathological inflammatory responses and compromise blood-brain barrier integrity, establishing potential conduits for neurodegeneration (Olsen & Singhrao, 2019; Dominy et al., 2019).

While high-resolution sequencing technologies and advanced computational frameworks have revolutionized microbial community interrogation (Callahan et al., 2016; Douglas et al., 2020; Caspi et al., 2020), existing studies investigating oral microbiome alterations in AD remain limited in scope. Most prior work has focused solely on taxonomic characterization without integrating ecological metrics, predicted functional profiling, or machine learning approaches to establish mechanistic links between oral dysbiosis and AD pathology. Furthermore, few studies have employed compositionally appropriate statistical methods or validated findings across multiple analytical modalities.

Here, we address these gaps through an integrated multi-omics approach characterizing oral microbiome dysbiosis in AD via systematic comparison of patients and age-matched controls. Our novel analytical framework combines high-resolution amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference, compositional statistical analysis using ANCOM-BC2 with centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation, predicted functional profiling via PICRUSt2, and supervised machine learning classification. This comprehensive strategy moves beyond simple correlation to establish mechanistic hypotheses regarding microbiome-host interactions in AD while identifying clinically translatable microbiome-derived biomarkers for neurodegenerative disease detection and monitoring.



METHODS

Fig. 1: Schematic overview of the bioinformatics analysis pipeline.

Study Design and Data Acquisition
Oral microbiome composition in AD patients and age-matched controls was investigated using Illumina 16S rRNA sequencing data from Vancouver, Canada (BioProject PRJNA770746). Raw data and metadata were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive. The analytical pipeline, including all scripts, is publicly available (https://github.com/ejtettevi/ad_oral_microbiome).

Data Preprocessing and Quality Control
Raw paired-end FASTQ files were downloaded using the SRA Toolkit (v3.0.0) with prefetch and fasterq-dump (Heldenbrand, 2017; Thornton, 2016). Initial quality control and adapter trimming were performed with fastp (v0.23.2) (Chen et al., 2018; Chen, 2023) (Fig. 1), using automatic adapter detection, per-base quality filtering (Q ≥ 20), and summary report generation. MultiQC was used for aggregate report visualization.

Further trimming was performed with cutadapt (v4.1) (Martin, 2011), removing Illumina adapter sequences (forward: AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCA; reverse: AGATCGGAAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT) and partial 16S primer sequences (8 bp of 341F and 806R: CCTACGGG, GACTACHV), with a minimum read length of 100 bp and maximum of 300 bp, a quality cutoff of Q20, and an error rate of 0.1. Trimmed reads were filtered for length and quality, and logs were reviewed for each sample.

Host DNA Decontamination
To minimize host DNA contamination, cutadapt-filtered reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg39) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5) with the - very-sensitive parameter (Langmead & Salzberg, 2012) (Fig. 1). Unmapped read pairs (representing microbial sequences) were extracted using SAMtools (v1.15) with flags -f 12 -F 256. Only samples with both host-free R1 and R2 files were retained for downstream analysis (Li et al., 2009).

Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV) Inference and Taxonomic Assignment
ASV inference was performed with DADA2 (v1.22.0) in R (v4.3.0), following best practices for high-resolution denoising (Callahan et al., 2016). The pipeline included: filtering and trimming (maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2,2), truncQ = 2), error rate learning, dereplication, paired-end denoising, merging, and chimera removal (consensus method, minFoldParentOverAbundance = 1.5). Singleton ASVs (appearing only once) were removed to reduce noise. Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA v138.1 database with assignTaxonomy and addSpecies functions (Quast et al., 2012). Representative ASV sequences were exported in FASTA format. Read tracking was performed at each step, and all summary tables were exported.

Diversity and Statistical Analyses
Alpha diversity metrics were computed using scikit-bio (v0.5.7) and vegan (v2.6-2) (Oksanen et al., 2007; Dixon, 2003; Rideout et al., 2015). Beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis and Jaccard distances. Ordination analyses (PCoA, NMDS) were performed using scikit-bio and scikit-learn (v1.1.2), with PERMANOVA (999 permutations) for group differences and PERMDISP for dispersion homogeneity (Pedregosa et al., 2011; Anderson, 2017). Significance was set at p < 0.05, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995; Lachenbruch, 1989). All analyses were conducted using R and Python scripts. Our primary alpha diversity profiling was calculated strictly at the genus level, as was beta diversity analysis (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and Jaccard distance) from CLR-transformed abundances.

Differential Abundance and Functional Profiling of Microbial Communities
Compositional differential abundance analysis was performed using ANCOM-BC2 (v2.0.0), the latest version that accounts for composition bias, with model formulas incorporating group and batch variables when available. We applied ANCOM-BC2 to raw count data across all five hierarchical taxonomic levels, using the control group as the baseline reference level. Effect sizes were reported as log-fold changes (LFC) representing true CLR-transformed effect sizes, along with raw p-values and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted q-values. Predicted metagenomic functional profiles were generated using PICRUSt2 (v2.5.1), with pathway annotation from the MetaCyc database. Importantly, these predictions represent inferred functional potential based on marker gene abundance, not actual gene expression levels. Group comparisons of pathway abundances were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test on CLR-transformed abundances.

Machine Learning Classification
To align with our compositional data analysis framework, we re-ran all supervised machine learning classification pipelines using CLR-transformed ASV abundances (utilizing a pseudo-count of 0.5 to resolve zero counts). Models (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Logistic Regression, SVM) were implemented in scikit-learn (v1.1.2) with nested cross-validation for hyperparameter optimization. Feature selection was performed within each fold using SelectFromModel. Model evaluation metrics included balanced accuracy, weighted F1-score, and ROC-AUC, with all preprocessing performed within the training folds to prevent data leakage. Random Forest feature importance scores were extracted and annotated with DADA2 taxonomy to identify top discriminating biomarkers.

Visualization and Reproducibility
All plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.3.6), seaborn (v0.11.2), matplotlib (v3.5.2), and pheatmap (v1.0.12) (Wickham, 2009; Waskom, 2021; Hunter, 2007; Kolde & Kolde, 2015). All analytical scripts, metadata, and processed data are available at https://github.com/ejtettevi/ad_oral_microbiome to ensure full reproducibility.


RESULTS
Oral microbiome composition revealed significant dysbiosis in AD cases compared to controls. At the phylum level (Fig. 2A), Proteobacteria was significantly enriched in AD (28.5% vs 23.8% in controls; ANCOM-BC2 LFC = +0.22, q = 0.041), while Actinobacteriota was markedly depleted (1.7% vs 4.4%; LFC = -0.92, q = 0.008). Firmicutes and Bacteroidota showed non-significant shifts (LFC = -0.05, q = 0.78 and LFC = +0.29, q = 0.12, respectively).

At finer taxonomic resolutions, we observed enrichment of potentially pathogenic taxa and depletion of beneficial groups. Class-level analysis (Fig. 2B) showed significant enrichment of Gammaproteobacteria (LFC = +0.22, q = 0.041) and depletion of Actinobacteria (LFC = -0.92, q = 0.008). Order-level analysis revealed notable enrichment of Enterobacteriales (LFC = +0.26, q = 0.023) and Staphylococcales (LFC = +0.62, q = 0.005), alongside depletion of Micrococcales (LFC = -1.05, q = 0.003) and Lactobacillales (LFC = -0.11, q = 0.32). Family-level analysis demonstrated enrichment of Pasteurellaceae (LFC = +0.26, q = 0.023) and Gemellaceae (LFC = +0.62, q = 0.005), with depletion of Micrococcaceae (LFC = -1.05, q = 0.003) and Streptococcaceae (LFC = -0.11, q = 0.32). At the genus level, Actinobacillus (LFC = +0.94, q = 0.012) and Gemella (LFC = +0.62, q = 0.005) were significantly enriched, while Rothia (LFC = -1.05, q = 0.003) and Actinomyces (LFC = -0.85, q = 0.009) were depleted.

Alpha diversity analyses confirmed significantly increased microbial community complexity in AD (Fig. 3). Chao1 estimator showed greater genus richness in cases (p = 0.003), while Fisher's diversity index indicated enhanced diversity incorporating both richness and evenness (p = 0.009). These findings were supported by observed diversity measurements (p = 0.003), indicating AD oral microbiomes exhibit both greater species richness and more equitable abundance distributions.

Beta diversity analyses demonstrated clear compositional and structural separation between groups (Fig. 4). Bray-Curtis PCoA explained 32.4% of total variance with significant group separation (PERMANOVA F = 8.42, p = 0.003). Cases displayed significantly greater multivariate dispersion (betadisper F = 6.87, p = 0.002), indicating heightened ecological heterogeneity and community destabilization in AD. Jaccard-based analyses corroborated these findings, showing significant compositional differences (PERMANOVA F = 11.23, p = 0.001) and dispersion differences (betadisper F = 9.56, p = 0.001).

Predictive functional profiling via PICRUSt2 revealed coordinated upregulation of biosynthetic pathways in AD-associated microbiomes (Fig. 5). The most pronounced increase occurred in peptidoglycan maturation (PWY0-1586), with 58% higher median abundance in cases versus controls (87,000 vs 55,000 units; p < 0.001). Six additional pathways showed robust and consistent upregulation: thiamine formation (PWY-7357), CDP-diacylglycerol biosynthesis I and II (PWY-5667, PWY0-1319), superpathway of tetrahydrofolate biosynthesis (PWY-6612), superpathway of phospholipid biosynthesis I (PHOLIPSYNT-PWY), and superpathway of tetrahydrofolate biosynthesis and salvage (FOLSYN-PWY). All significantly altered pathways exhibited log₂ fold changes between 0.35 and 0.45 (1.3-1.4-fold increases), with the six most significant showing clear separation between cases and controls (fold changes 1.36-1.38; p < 0.01). This systematic upregulation pattern suggests activation of higher-order regulatory mechanisms driving metabolic reprogramming in disease.

Machine learning classification using CLR-transformed ASV abundances demonstrated strong diagnostic potential of oral microbiome signatures. Random Forest achieved optimal performance with AUC = 0.85 (95% CI: 0.72-0.98), outperforming Gradient Boosting (AUC = 0.77), Logistic Regression (AUC = 0.75), and SVM (AUC = 0.69). Confusion matrix analysis revealed 77.8% sensitivity and 81.8% specificity for Random Forest. Feature importance analysis identified ASV4 (Gemella) as the strongest discriminating biomarker (importance score = 0.035), followed by ASV2 (Haemophilus) and ASV42 (Neisseria oralis).

DISCUSSION
The present study provides a comprehensive, multi-layered characterization of oral microbiome dysbiosis associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD), revealing significant taxonomic restructuring, ecological destabilization, and coordinated metabolic reprogramming that collectively suggest a fundamental alteration in host-microbe homeostasis with potential pathophysiological relevance to neurodegeneration.

At the taxonomic level, our findings extend prior observations of Proteobacteria expansion and Actinobacteriota depletion in AD by providing compositionally rigorous effect sizes using ANCOM-BC2. The 20% relative increase in Proteobacteria and 61% decrease in Actinobacteriota represent substantial shifts in community architecture that align with, yet quantify more precisely than, previous reports linking oral dysbiosis to neurodegenerative conditions (Dominy et al., 2019; Olsen & Singhrao, 2019; Olsen & Singhrao, 2015). Notably, these phylum-level changes appear driven by specific lineage expansions—particularly Gammaproteobacteria and Bacilli—suggesting targeted ecological succession rather than random community fluctuation (Kilian et al., 2016).

Moving beyond taxonomic description, our order- and family-level analyses reveal a coherent ecological transition consistent with the loss of oral microbial homeostasis hypothesis. The depletion of known beneficial taxa (Lactobacillales and Streptococcaceae) concurrent with enrichment of potentially pathogenic groups (Enterobacteriales, Staphylococcales, Pasteurellaceae, and Gemellaceae) suggests a shift from a homeostatic, symbiotic community structure toward one enriched in taxa with pro-inflammatory potential. This restructuring creates a biological plausibility for oral microbiome contributions to systemic inflammation and neuroimmune activation in AD (Long et al., 2022a).

Our diversity findings challenge conventional dysbiosis paradigms. The significant elevation in alpha diversity indices (Chao1 p=0.003; Fisher p=0.009) indicates increased rather than decreased community complexity in AD-associated oral microbiomes. This observation aligns with emerging ecological theories of dysbiosis as a state of community destabilization and functional redundancy loss, rather than mere simplification. The concurrent increase in beta diversity dispersion further supports this interpretation, suggesting heterogeneous microbial responses to AD-related host physiological changes that reduce community stability and resistance to perturbation (Tang et al., 2016; Anderson, 2017; Zhu et al., 2025).

Perhaps most mechanistically informative, our predictive functional profiling reveals a coordinated upregulation of biosynthetic pathways in AD-associated microbiomes. The 58% increase in peptidoglycan maturation pathway abundance—coupled with consistent 1.3-1.4-fold elevations in thiamine formation, phospholipid biosynthesis, and tetrahydrofolate pathways—suggests systemic metabolic reprogramming rather than random perturbation. This coordinated response implies activation of higher-order regulatory mechanisms driving increased global metabolic flux, potentially reflecting microbial adaptation to altered host microenvironmental conditions in AD (Douglas et al., 2020; Caspi et al., 2020). Importantly, these functional shifts were detectable using only 16S rRNA data, establishing a foundation for non-invasive biomarker development.

Critically, our machine learning approach demonstrates that these multi-layered alterations possess tangible diagnostic utility. The Random Forest classifier's AUC of 0.85 using CLR-transformed taxonomic and functional features significantly exceeds chance performance and compares favorably to established AD biomarkers. The identification of specific discriminating taxa—particularly Gemella (ASV4), Haemophilus (ASV2), and Neisseria oralis (ASV42)—provides concrete targets for future validation studies. These findings underscore that oral microbiome signatures capture biologically relevant information distinguishing AD from healthy aging, with potential applications in early detection, disease stratification, or treatment monitoring.

Despite these advances, several limitations contextualize our findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding whether observed microbiome alterations drive or result from AD pathogenesis. Technical constraints inherent to 16S rRNA sequencing—including limited taxonomic resolution and functional prediction accuracy—necessitate cautious interpretation, particularly regarding species-level associations and metabolic activity. Furthermore, while we controlled for major demographic variables, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., detailed medication histories, precise dietary patterns, periodontal status) remains possible.

Future research should prioritize several directions to build upon this foundation. Longitudinal studies tracking microbiome changes relative to cognitive decline would establish temporal relationships and potential predictive value. Multi-omics integration—particularly metatranscriptomics and metabolomics—is essential to validate predicted functional shifts and identify active biochemical pathways. Intervention studies modulating the oral microbiome (e.g., via targeted antimicrobials, probiotics, or periodontal treatment) could test causality and therapeutic potential. Finally, expanding to more diverse populations and incorporating detailed clinical phenotyping will enhance generalizability and enable subtype-specific analyses.

In summary, our integrated analysis demonstrates that oral microbiome dysbiosis in AD represents a coherent, multi-system alteration encompassing taxonomic shifts, ecological destabilization, and metabolic reprogramming. These changes are not merely correlative but suggest biologically plausible mechanisms linking oral microbial communities to neurodegenerative processes. The strong diagnostic performance of machine learning models further supports translational potential, positioning oral microbiome signatures as accessible, non-invasive biomarkers worthy of continued investigation in the pursuit of improved AD detection, understanding, and ultimately, intervention.

CONCLUSION
This study integrates high-resolution taxonomic, ecological, and predicted functional analyses to characterize oral microbiome dysbiosis in AD. AD is associated with distinct microbial community restructuring, increased diversity, and upregulation of biosynthetic and energy metabolism pathways at the predicted functional potential level. The robust performance of machine learning classifiers underscores the translational potential of oral microbiome signatures as non-invasive biomarkers for AD diagnosis and monitoring. Further validation with metagenomic, metabolomic, and longitudinal data is warranted.
